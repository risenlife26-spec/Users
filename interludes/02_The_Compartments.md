# INTERLUDE

## The Compartments

---

the Grapevine, the white Silverado sat high in the equipment yard on Mickey Thompson tires that still smelled like rubber and dealership Armor All. Blake was behind the wheel in his orange-mirrored Oakleys and in each lens the yard and the men and the chain-link fence were warped together into a single distorted image he had no interest in examining.

Jaime shuffled across the dust in a Dodgers cap pulled low, shoulders already rounded like he knew what was coming. He moved like an apology. He stepped in close to the open window, resting his elbows on the door frame out of habit, fingers laced over the bill of his hat. His dirt-caked hands hovered right over Blake’s lap.

“Back up, fool.”

Jaime jerked his hands away. “Oh, perdón, jefe.”

Blake’s eyes went to the faint smudge of dust on the new paint. Third time late this week. Now smudging his truck. It was always something. You let one thing slide and it spread. One guy figures ten minutes doesn’t matter, then it’s fifteen, then twenty, and pretty soon you don’t have a crew. You have a suggestion.

“This is a new truck, pendejo. Don’t be putting your dirty hands on it.”

Heat flickered across Jaime’s face.

“Got it, jefe. It won’t happen again. I’m sorry I was late. My truck was out of gas. My brother gave me ten dollars but the pump on Chester was down.”

There it was. The story. They all had one. Gas money. Sick kid. Broken-down car. Blake could write half of them himself by now. What they never said was the thing that mattered: I messed up. I chose wrong. I didn’t plan ahead. That was the rot. Nobody wanting to own the simple truth that they’d screwed up.

And Blake — Blake could see the rot in every man he supervised and could not see it in the mirror he was wearing on his face. The Oakleys reflected the yard and the men and the chain-link fence but they did not reflect Blake. That was the function of the mirrored lens. The mirrored lens was the first compartment he put on every morning. It said: I see you. You don’t see me. The seeing without being seen was the power and the power was the pride and the pride was the disease and Blake had been terminal since the third row of the Church of Christ on Union Avenue where he’d learned to move his mouth through the hymn words and produce the sound of worship without any of the substance.

“How many times, Jaime?” Blake turned to the foreman beside him. “Can you believe this guy?”

He slapped the foreman on the arm. Just enough to pull a chuckle out of the man.

“I, I — sorry, jef—”

“Don’t grovel.” The stammer scraped at Blake’s nerves. Groveling set his teeth on edge. His old man had been the same way. If you’re going to be useless, at least be useless standing up. “Be a man and take responsibility.”

Jaime’s mouth closed.

“Say you slept in late,” Blake said. He heard his own voice sharpening, finding the edge he knew how to use. “Don’t give me the bullshit about gas. Tell me it was a hangover or you’re fired.”

The yard held its breath. Behind Jaime, two men slowed their work without looking over. The foreman’s silence beside Blake was a question Blake didn’t answer.

Jaime stared at the ground. In the mirrored orange of Blake’s Oakleys, his small figure floated — hat, sloped shoulders, the bow of his neck. He looked like a kid waiting for a belt.

“I was hungover,” Jaime whispered.

Blake let a beat pass. There it was. The admission. The truth — but only when cornered.

“Well, see, that doesn’t work for me. Because you only told the truth once I pressed you against the wall. You tried to hide behind a story first.”

He looked at the foreman. Making sure the lesson landed.

“I can’t employ cowards or liars,” Blake said. Each word clean and measured. “You’re fired.”

Jaime didn’t argue. They rarely did once you pressed them against their own words. He stood there a moment, hat brim hiding his eyes, then turned away across the yard.

In Blake’s chest, a small tight satisfaction settled. You did what you had to do. Someone had to be the one who didn’t blink.

He adjusted his Oakleys and caught his own reflection — jaw clenched, mouth in the flat line he associated with being in charge. He liked that look. It made him feel solid. Like his edges were clear.

But the reflection in the Oakleys was a reflection of a reflection — twice removed from the face, twice filtered, the image of a man who had just demanded honesty from a hungover kid while carrying a phone in his pocket with two conversations that would have ended his engagement, his reputation, and the moral authority he’d just used to fire a man for lying. The Oakleys showed Blake a man of principle. The phone showed the truth. And the phone was in the same pocket as the keys to the motel room he’d visit in four hours.

The pocket held both. Blake held neither up to the light.

The truck rolled through the gate as the sky turned the color of dirty dishwater.

Blake thumbed his phone and hit the foreman’s number. It rang once.

“Yeah.”

“It had to be done,” Blake said. “You know that, right?”

Silence. Engine noise on both ends.

“Jaime,” Blake said. “I’m not running a charity. Third time late in a week? You start looking the other way on that, pretty soon the whole outfit’s dragging in at seven forty-five, eight o’clock. That’s how you lose a job, not keep one.”

“Mm,” the foreman said.

Guilt had sat in Blake’s chest all afternoon like a swallowed stone. It wasn’t a feeling he was willing to have.

“Listen,” he said, and his voice shifted into the tone he used in toolbox talks, the tone that made men look up from their coffee. “A man’s got one thing in this world that nobody can take from him but he can give away — his word.”

The foreman didn’t answer. Blake filled the silence.

“You got your body, sure. That goes. Gets old, gets tired, blows out a knee, back goes bad. You got your skills, but those are rented. Somebody always younger, cheaper, hungrier. The only thing that’s actually yours is what you say you’re gonna do.” He tapped the steering wheel with two fingers. “You say you’re gonna be there at seven? You’re there at seven. Not seven-ten, not seven-oh-five with a story about gas. Seven.”

He warmed to it. He warmed to it the way a preacher warms to a sermon he’s given before — the words coming easy, the rhythm smooth, the conviction manufactured fresh each time from the same blueprint because the blueprint was the compartment and the compartment produced conviction on demand. Blake’s voice on the phone to the foreman was the voice of a man who believed what he was saying. And he did believe it. That was the horror. The compartments didn’t require him to disbelieve the principles. The compartments allowed him to believe the principles completely in one room while violating them completely in another and the believing and the violating never touched because the doors were locked and the architect was good at his job.

“A man who can’t keep time can’t keep anything. Can’t keep a job, can’t keep a family, can’t keep his own head straight. You start letting yourself off the hook with little lies — the pump was down, my brother didn’t show, traffic was bad — and pretty soon there’s nothing left holding you upright.”

A text banner slid across the top of his phone screen:

You still coming baby? 💋

Another line pulsed underneath it.

He ignored it. Eyes on the road.

“You saw him,” Blake said. “First thing out of his mouth is a story. That’s not a man. That’s a child. And I’m not in the business of raising other men’s sons.”

The phone buzzed again:

Need to know before I shower 😘

Blake changed lanes.

“You know how many men I’ve watched talk big about being good providers and then they’re off drinking, sleeping in, leaving their kids waiting on the porch? You either are what you say you are, or you’re not. No middle. That’s why I don’t feel bad about Jaime. He had chances. He chose.”

He believed that. Or he needed to. The difference between believing and needing to believe was the difference between the man in the Oakleys and the man behind the Oakleys and Blake had spent so long wearing the Oakleys that he could no longer tell which man was talking.

“If anyone asks, you tell them the truth: he couldn’t keep his word. That’s all this is. It’s not personal.”

The foreman let out a breath. “Sure,” he said quietly. “Not personal.”

The call clicked off.

Blake picked up the phone and opened the texts.

You still coming baby? 💋

Need to know before I shower 😘

He typed: 20 mins. Be ready.

His thumb hovered. He added: Don’t start without me.

He hit send. The anticipation came in low and warm and flattened the guilt under it. He pictured the room. 14B. Ground floor, end of the row, the door that stuck if you didn’t lift the handle. The smell of the place — carpet cleaner and something floral from the dispenser in the bathroom, and underneath both of those the permanent motel smell that no cleaner had ever killed. She’d have the TV on low. She always had the TV on low. And she’d be at the door before he knocked because she watched for his headlights through the gap in the curtain.

Sarah would be at the apartment by now. His fiancée. The image of her came with its familiar weight — mouth set, eyes already scanning him for the ways he’d come up short before he’d even opened his mouth. She’d ask if he’d called about the insurance. If he’d looked at the catering estimate for the wedding. If he’d followed up with the venue about the deposit. She’d say he was never present. Never keeping his promises.

Promises. The word slid past him and kept going.

He gripped the wheel tighter. Heading not toward the apartment but toward the turnoff that led to the cheap motel behind the gas station. The place he used because it was out of the way. Forgettable.

He told himself what he always told himself: if Sarah had any idea what it meant to shoulder what he shouldered, to hold it all together, she might talk to him like he was worth something. Like he was a man whose word mattered.

She didn’t. So he went where he did get what he deserved.

What he deserved. The phrase sat in the sentence like a loaded gun with the safety off. Blake used it the way every man in the architecture of pride used it — as currency, as justification, as the ledger that said: I have worked, I have carried, I have endured, and therefore the debt the world owes me can be collected at a motel on the south side of town. The deserving was the root. Not the woman. Not the room. Not the lie he’d tell Sarah. The deserving. The conviction that a man who carries enough weight earns the right to set it down wherever he wants, and the wherever was 14B, and the setting-down was the sin, and the sin was not the act. The sin was the pride that preceded the act — the pride that said I have earned this when the truth was that the only thing Blake had earned was the distance between himself and every person he claimed to love.

He didn’t think about the things he’d said to her last week — promises to cut back on hours, to be home for dinner, to turn over a new leaf. He’d meant them in the moment. That counted, in his mind. You couldn’t be faulted for life getting in the way.

But a man who says seven and shows at seven — that mattered. That was different.

The motel’s faded sign appeared ahead. One letter flickering.

Hurry. I miss you.

Blake turned into the lot. The words he’d spoken to the foreman were still in his ears, clean and righteous:

Your word is who you are.

He didn’t hold them up against the text thread on his screen. Or the lie he’d tell Sarah when she asked why he was late. Those things lived in different compartments. Tidy. Separate. In one compartment, he was a man of principle teaching hard lessons. In the other, he was just taking what he’d earned.

The compartments had doors. The doors had locks. Blake had been building them his whole life. He was the architect. He was the locksmith. He was the man who walked through one door and shut it behind him and opened the next, and the shutting and the opening happened so fast that no light from one room ever leaked into the other.

The motel room was dim. The woman at the door was smiling. The phone in his pocket held two conversations — one with a foreman about integrity, one with a woman who wasn’t his fiancée — and the phone didn’t know the difference. The phone held both.

The phone was the most honest thing Blake owned.

Because the phone didn’t have compartments.

He left the motel at ten-fourteen. The woman was asleep. The room smelled like her shampoo and the carpet cleaner and something else he didn’t want to name. He pulled the door shut behind him, lifting the handle as he always did, and walked to the Silverado in the parking lot.

Titus heard the truck before Blake killed the engine.

The bark came once — deep, singular, the sound of a foundation settling — and then the gate was shaking and Blake could see the shape of him through the chain-link, the massive black head, the ears up, the tail going.

He unlatched the gate. Titus hit him chest-high, a hundred thirty pounds of muscle and need. The paws on his shoulders. The tongue on his jaw. The force of the greeting knocked Blake back a step and he grabbed the dog’s collar and pushed him down.

“Off, Titus. Off.”

The dog dropped. Sat. His entire back end wagged so hard his hindquarters lifted off the concrete with each swing. Those brown eyes — wide, unblinking, locked on Blake’s face with an intensity that had nothing to do with hunger and everything to do with the fact that Blake had been gone for sixteen hours and the dog had been counting every one.

He’d left at six that morning. Sixteen hours. The water trough was low. The food bowl was licked dry and pushed halfway across the yard. The shade tarp had come loose on one side and was flapping in the evening wind.

Blake filled both bowls. Titus ate fast, loud, the metal bowl scraping concrete, the sound of a creature who believed every meal might be his last. When he finished he came to where Blake was sitting on the back step and laid his head on Blake’s boot.

The weight of it. Five pounds of skull and jaw and trust resting on the leather and the separating sole and the foot inside that had walked into a motel room two hours ago and walked out smelling like another woman’s shampoo.

The dog didn’t know. The dog didn’t care. The dog just rested his head on the boot and breathed and the breathing was steady and even and asked for nothing.

And that was the thing Blake couldn’t build a compartment for. Not the motel, not the lie, not the sermon to the foreman, not the text thread, not Sarah’s scanning eyes. Those fit in the compartments. Those had doors and locks and addresses. But the dog on his boot did not fit because the dog was not transactional. The dog had not earned Blake. The dog had not been earned by Blake. The dog had no ledger. The dog had no economy. The dog had a boot and a jaw and a pair of brown eyes that said: You are here. I am here. The here is the whole thing. And the here — the absolute, unreasonable, undeserved here of a hundred-thirty-pound animal pressing its skull into the boot of a man who had just driven home from a motel where he’d betrayed every principle he’d preached that afternoon — the here was the closest thing to grace Blake had ever felt and he could not receive it because receiving it would have required admitting he didn’t deserve it and the admitting was the one compartment he would not open.

“I’m a piece of shit, Titus.”

The dog’s tail thumped once against the concrete. Slow. Deliberate.

Blake scratched behind his ears — that spot where the short black fur gave way to the softer skin underneath, the spot that made the dog lean into his hand with his full weight, eyes closing, the hundred-thirty-pound body going slack with something that could only be called trust.

The motel room was in one compartment. The yard crew was in another. Sarah was in a third. But the dog didn’t live in any of them. The dog lived in the space between — the hallway that connected every room, the corridor that Blake walked through twenty times a day pretending the doors on either side were sealed.

The dog knew the hallway. The dog slept in it. The dog was the only living thing that saw Blake move from room to room and never asked which one was real.

They were all real. That was what the dog knew and Blake didn’t. The compartments were not separate rooms. They were one room with imaginary walls and the walls were made of pride and the pride was made of the conviction that Blake was the architect and the architect was in control and the control was the lie and the lie was that a man who builds his own rooms gets to decide which room he’s in. He doesn’t. He’s in all of them at once. The dog knew this. The dog slept in the hallway because the hallway was the only honest place in the house.

Blake sat on the step with the dog’s head on his boot and the Valley dark around him and the stars coming out over the oil fields to the north and the almond orchards to the west and he did not move.

The phone buzzed in his pocket.

He didn’t look at it.

The dog breathed. The stars turned. The compartments held.

But the hallway was getting longer. The dog was pacing more. The doors were getting harder to close. Somewhere in the architecture of Blake Wexler’s pride, a crack was forming — not in the walls, not in the doors, but in the foundation. The foundation was the conviction that the architect was good at his job. The foundation was cracking because the architect was sitting on a step at ten-thirty at night with a dog on his boot and the smell of another woman’s shampoo on his collar and the words your word is who you are in his ears and the words were true and the truth was that Blake’s word was worthless and Blake knew it and the knowing was the crack and the crack was the beginning and the beginning was the thing that would take two years and a blown radiator on the Grapevine and a man in a Ford with a tres leches cake to finish.

The dog breathed. The crack held.

The crack always held.

# CHAPTER 04 // THE HANDS

## MANUSCRIPT

The third Tuesday he came for the dog, Brittni was stitching a laceration on a terrier's ear and Cody sat in the plastic chair and watched her hands.

He didn't watch the way men watched. He tracked the hands. The way she held the needle driver — thumb and ring finger in the loops, index finger on the shaft for stability. He watched the needle enter the skin and pull through and the thread follow and the knot cinch and the ear close like a sentence finding its period.

"You're staring," Brittni said. She didn't look up from the ear.

"I'm learning."

"What are you learning."

"How your hands know where to go."

The interval between the needle entering and pulling through lengthened by a fraction, a half-beat.

"Muscle memory," she said. "Ten thousand hours."

"That's not what I'm looking at."

She looked up. He was sitting with his elbows on his knees, his hands hanging between them — the swollen knuckles, the bruised metacarpals, the chronic damage. His hands at rest looked like tools returned to a shelf after heavy use, set down but not put away, still warm from the work. He was looking at her with the flat direct focus, but the focus had changed. It was no longer assessing. It was attending.

She released the terrier to its owner — a woman from south Chester who carried the dog in a pillowcase because she didn't have a carrier and the pillowcase was the gentlest thing she owned.

The woman counted quarters from a Ziploc bag onto the counter. Her hands shook. The quarters clicked against the Formica and some of them rolled and Brittni said "It's fine, don't worry about it" and the woman kept counting.

Cody stood. He walked to the counter. He didn't look at the woman's face and he didn't look at her quarters and he didn't look at Brittni. He picked up the quarters that had rolled onto the floor — three of them, seventy-five cents — and set them on the counter next to the woman's pile without comment. Then he walked to the door and held it open for her. She passed through with the dog in the pillowcase. He closed it behind her.

He sat back down in the plastic chair.

Brittni looked at the three quarters on the counter. She did not say thank you.

"You want to get food," he said. Not a question. Not a command. The grammar of a man who did not know how to ask but could not tell.

"Yeah," Brittni said. "I want to get food."

They ate at a taco truck on White Lane four blocks from the clinic. Al pastor and horchata on the curb. Cody ate fast, with his hands, the salsa running down his wrist. Brittni ate methodically, napkin on her knee. They sat on the curb and the traffic passed and the streetlights were on and the gap between the lights started where the tire shop ended and the gap was dark.

He told her about the beach. Not the punch. Not Mickey. He told her about the girl and the sleeping bag and the four days. He told it the way he told everything — as weather. Clear skies over Lahaina.

He told her the girl had put her hand on his chest while she slept and when he shifted she adjusted her hand without waking. He said it looking at the taco wrapper in his fist.

Her hand — the one not holding the cup — was resting on the curb between them. She did not move it toward him. She did not move it away.

Cody looked at the hand on the curb. He looked at it the way he had watched her stitch. Attending.

He set the taco wrapper down. He placed his hand on the curb next to hers. Not touching. An inch of concrete between his knuckle and her pinkie. The inch was a question and the question was the most articulate thing Cody had ever said.

Brittni closed the inch.

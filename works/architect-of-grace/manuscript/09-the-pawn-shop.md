# CHAPTER 09 // THE PAWN SHOP

## MANUSCRIPT

Blake found the sidewalk on a Thursday. Three weeks after the funeral. He drove to White Lane in the Silverado. He parked in front of the panadería. Got out. Stood on the curb and looked south toward the gap between the streetlights where Cody had died.

The sidewalk had been power-washed. He could see the clean patch — a rectangle of lighter concrete, maybe six by eight, the pressure washer's arc still visible in the grain. Around the edges the old concrete was dark with years of Valley grime. But the rectangle was clean. Somebody from the city had come out with a truck and a hose and blasted the blood off the concrete and left.

Blake stood over the rectangle. His boots on the edge of it.

He didn't know what he'd expected. He hadn't come for Lisa's clinic. He'd come for the sidewalk. The police had told him nothing. One round. Left chest. The coroner's report said .38 or .357, consistent with a revolver. No shell casing recovered because revolvers don't eject casings.

The pawn shop on Union Avenue was called Gold Country. Blake walked in. The bell rang — a real bell, brass, mounted on a spring above the door. The interior was fluorescent-lit and smelled like gun oil and dust.

The gun case was against the back wall. Twelve handguns under glass, arranged by caliber. Blake looked at them.

The man behind the counter unlocked the case and pulled two revolvers and laid them on the glass mat. A Smith & Wesson Model 10 with a four-inch barrel, blued, the grips worn smooth. And a Charter Arms Undercover, snub-nose.

"The Smith's a department trade-in. Three hundred. The Charter's two-twenty but the trigger pull's heavy."

Blake picked up the Smith. The weight of it. He opened the cylinder. Empty. Five chambers, clean. He closed it. The click was small and final — the sound of a thing locking into the position it was designed to occupy.

"You got a preference?" the man said.

Blake looked at the Smith in his hand. It was the same caliber that had killed his cousin on a sidewalk four blocks from a barbecue restaurant. It was the same caliber Rick had used in a Delano garage twenty years ago.

"This one," Blake said.

He filled out the paperwork. California DROS form. Ten-day waiting period. He put the deposit down in cash. The man wrote the receipt by hand on a carbon pad and tore off the yellow copy and slid it across the counter.

"Ten days," the man said. "You know the drill."

Blake walked out into the Union Avenue heat. The Silverado was parked at the curb. Titus was standing in the bed, ears forward, watching Blake come through the door.

Blake put his hand on the dog's head. The skull was warm from the sun. "Ten days, Titus."

He got in the truck. He sat behind the wheel with the engine off and the heat building and the receipt in his pocket and the clean rectangle of sidewalk four miles south and the notebook in his back pocket and the poem inside the notebook and the tear stain on the poem and the thief on the cross and the open hands and the .38 he couldn't pick up for ten days and the question he couldn't put down for ten seconds:

*Who killed Cody with a .38 revolver on White Lane in the dark and why were his hands open?*

He started the truck. He drove south on Union. Past the check-cashing places and the tire shops and the taco trucks and the Dollar General and the church on Chester Avenue where the marquee said GOD'S TIMING IS PERFECT in black letters on a white board and Blake looked at the marquee and his jaw worked once and he kept driving.

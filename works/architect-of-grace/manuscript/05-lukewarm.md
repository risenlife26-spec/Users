# CHAPTER 05 // LUKEWARM

## MANUSCRIPT

Joe's grow was a corrugated steel outbuilding off 7th Standard Road where the county line blurred into pump jacks and diesel exhaust and dirt that hadn't grown anything on its own in forty years.

Inside was a jungle built on math. Sixty thousand watts of high-pressure sodium light. Four hundred plants in ten-gallon fabric pots arranged in rows on rolling tables. The light was violently orange, thick and synthetic, throwing no shadows. The ballasts hummed at a frequency that bypassed the ears and sat in the molars.

Blake maintained the systems. He calibrated the nutrient dosers and checked the pH levels in the reservoir tanks. The room was binary — a pump pulled water or it didn't, a circuit was closed or open — and the binary was the only honest thing in his life because it didn't require him to choose a side.

He had been living in the straddle for three years.

His left boot scraped the wet concrete as he shifted his weight. The sole of the Tony Lama had separated another half-inch since the Grapevine. Every step produced a sound — scuff-slap, scuff-slap. Instead of taking them to be stitched, Blake had wrapped a band of silver duct tape around the instep. A patch. A half-measure to keep the thing walking without fixing it. The duct tape was the straddle made visible.

It was a Tuesday in November. The Valley had cooled. The orange light pressed down on Blake's shoulders as he bled an airlock out of the main irrigation manifold. His hands were wet. His knuckles smeared with purple PVC primer. The ballasts hummed in his teeth.

His mother's voice arrived without invitation. Diane at the kitchen table on Brundage Lane, reading aloud while Blake ate cereal before school, her finger on the page, her voice carrying the flat Bakersfield cadence that made scripture sound like directions to the hardware store.

*I know your deeds, that you are neither cold nor hot. I wish you were either one or the other. So, because you are lukewarm — neither hot nor cold — I will spit you out of my mouth.*

Standing in the orange light with PVC primer on his hands and a separating boot sole and a compartment for the grow room and a compartment for the landscaping yard and a compartment for the woman on White Lane and a compartment for his cousin's swollen knuckles — standing in the straddle at eleven-forty on a Tuesday night, the verse did not sound like background noise.

It sounded like a diagnosis.

The pressure behind his sternum spiked. A physical compression — the iron walls between the compartments buckling under load. The guilt of his want for Lisa bled into the violence of Cody's knuckles bled into the quiet of the man at the gas pump bled into the orange light and the forced growth and the duct tape holding his boot together, a series of patches over separating soles, half-measures stacked on half-measures, and the stack was collapsing.

His shoulders curled inward. His jaw locked. His hands, holding the red-handled PVC cutters, closed into tight fists. The body folding in on itself — making itself small enough to survive the vacuum.

He couldn't breathe. The orange light smeared. The sixty-hertz hum became a roar.

Blake dropped the PVC cutters. They hit the wet concrete.

He went to his knees. The duct tape on his left boot scraped the floor. He knelt in the spilled water and the purple primer and the smell of the forced harvest. His hands were still clenched. His jaw still locked. Every reflex trying to hold the architecture together — seal the compartments, maintain the straddle, keep the fourteen feet.

He fought it. He fought the fists. He forced his fingers to uncurl — one at a time, each one a door opening onto a room he had kept locked for years. He laid his hands flat on his thighs. Palms up. He dropped his shoulders. He let the perimeter collapse.

The air hit him.

The detonation of a held breath — the crack in the chest when the thing you've been gripping lets go and the letting go is so sudden and so total that the body doesn't know what to do with the space. The heavy weight settling. The floor beneath his knees catching him the way bedrock catches a footer.

He was not holding himself up. The floor was holding him.

Blake knelt in the grow room and wept. It was ugly and absolute — the weeping of a man who has been pressing against a door for twenty-six years and is finally dragged through it. Not the clean kind. Not the baptism-video kind. The kind where a man's failures are not the minor missteps of a distracted life but structural collapses. He knelt with his hands open and the water soaking through his jeans and the purple primer drying on his knuckles and he let his lukewarm life burn to the ground.

He got in the truck. He pulled the door shut. He drove west toward White Lane, toward the intersection where the streetlights started and the dark began.

# CHAPTER 02 // THE PROSPECT

## MANUSCRIPT

The room was cinder block and had no windows on the street side. Edison Highway was thirty yards beyond the tire shop but you couldn't hear it in here. The door was steel. The lock was a deadbolt and a chain and the chain was ceremonial — the building had never been tested by someone who didn't belong because the building was known, and the knowing was the lock.

Oak table. Eight feet long. Burn marks from cigarettes that predated the ban. Seven chairs.

Seven men sat at the table. Their cuts hung on the backs of their chairs or stayed on their shoulders. The leather was old. The leather had weight. Not the weight of cowhide — the weight of use, of road, of years spent absorbing the oil and the sweat and the interstate grime that worked into the grain until the grain was indistinguishable from the man who wore it.

The Cut, a vestment. A sacramental garment for men ordained into a priesthood that acknowledged no God and served no heaven and operated on one doctrine: loyalty — total, absolute — was the only virtue, and breaking it was the only sin. A Christian wears a cross to ward off evil. The Cut warded off the good. It sealed out mercy. It sealed out tenderness. It sealed out the part of the world that might have saved the man wearing it, and the sealing was the sacrament. That was the point. That was always the point.

Rick sat at the head of the table. The gavel in front of him. Oak handle, brass head, eleven years in his grip. He picked it up. He brought it down once.

"Church is open."

The sound was a flat wooden crack that did not echo because cinder block absorbs.

They went through dues. Cody poured. He came through the side door with a case of Budweiser from the refrigerator and he set a can at each man's right hand without being asked and without making eye contact and without pausing between the settings. Seven cans. Seven right hands. The placement was precise. Cody's hands remembered where every man sat because Cody's hands had been placing cans at this table for nine months and the nine months had trained the hands into automation, into ritual, into the muscle memory of a man whose service had become indistinguishable from his skeleton.

Rick called for the prospect report. Cody stepped forward. He reported on Laughlin. Declarative, compressed, no adjectives.

He paused. "There was a thing at the bar."

Rick waited.

"Vagos hang-around at the Riverside. Running his mouth about the California rocker. I handled it."

"How."

"Put him on the ground. One shot. He stayed down."

Sal grunted. "Kid hits like a truck."

Cody received the observation. He did not smile. He did not nod. He stood at the end of the table with his hands at his sides and his prospect cut on his shoulders — the bottom rocker, the MC patch, the empty space on the back where the Death's Head would go — and he received the compliment without opinion, without negotiation, without the small human flinch that separates a man who does violence from a man who is violence.

"Go back to the door," Rick said.

Cody went back to the door. He stood against the steel. His hands at his sides. His chin level. His weight even.

Rick held the gavel and watched the boy at the door. The kid's hands at his sides. The face that gave nothing. The one-shot report. *Put him on the ground. He stayed down.* No editorial. No aftermath. No flinch.

Rick watched Cody wipe the table — the rag moving in even strokes, the acolyte maintaining the altar, the body offered to the function without remainder. The kid served the club with the same absolute surrender Rick had watched in men who served God. The mechanism was identical. The direction was the only difference. And the direction was the only thing that mattered.

If Cody's fire were pointed at Christ, he would be a saint. He would storm the gates of hell with a Bible and a push broom. He would burn for God with the same totality he burned for this table. The combustion was the gift. The fuel was the gift. The absolute refusal to half-step was the gift.

But the gift was pointed here. At the gavel. At the Cut. At the cinder-block cathedral and its priesthood of leather and its doctrine of loyalty and its sacrament of sealed-out mercy. And the gift, pointed here, would consume the boy whole.

"Last item," Rick said. His voice carried the room. "The vote on the Pismo sweep is Saturday at noon. Anybody not here doesn't ride. Anything else for the table?"

Silence. Seven men. Seven cuts.

"Church is closed."

He brought the gavel down. The brass head struck the oak. The sound was small and flat and the room absorbed it and the cinder block held it and the men stood and the chairs scraped and the cuts came off the chairbacks and went onto the shoulders.

Cody held the door. He held it for every man who walked through it. He held it with his arm straight and his feet planted and he did not move until the last patched member passed and then he stepped through himself and the door closed behind him and the room was empty.

Rick set the gavel on the table. Brass on oak. The click was quiet. He sat in the empty room and he did not move.

The radiator blew at mile marker sixty-three on the northbound 5 and Blake pulled the Silverado onto the shoulder and watched the steam come through the hood in a white column that bent east in the wind and dissolved against the brown hills.

September. Hundred and twelve degrees on the Grapevine. The asphalt shimmered in both directions and the shimmer made the road look wet and the wetness was a lie the Valley told every summer — the promise of water where there was no water, the mirage that kept you driving toward something that wasn't there.

He turned the key off. The engine ticked as the metal contracted. The cab was already hot and getting hotter. He had no water. He had half a pack of Marlboro Reds and a phone with eleven percent battery and an expired AAA card in the glovebox he'd been meaning to renew since March.

He got out. The heat hit him like opening an oven door. The shoulder gravel crunched under his boots — Tony Lamas, square toe, the left sole separating at the heel. He walked to the front of the truck and looked at the steam still threading up from under the hood and didn't open it because opening a blown radiator at a hundred and twelve degrees was something a man did once and then never did again and Blake had done it once, in Oildale, when he was nineteen, and the scar on the inside of his left forearm was still there, shiny and tight, a strip of skin that had been cooked and had healed into something smoother than the skin around it, as if the burning had simplified it.

He lit a cigarette. He stood on the shoulder of Interstate 5 at the foot of the Tehachapi Mountains and smoked and watched the trucks come up the grade in the slow lane, loaded, the diesels working.

Nobody stopped. He didn't expect them to. A man standing next to a white Silverado on the shoulder of the 5 in September could be anything — broken down, drunk, armed, wanted. The trucks didn't stop for anything that wasn't a weigh station or a woman. Blake didn't blame them. He wouldn't have stopped either.

He thought about Titus. The dog would be pacing by now. Blake had left him in the yard with the water trough full and the shade tarp staked over the concrete pad, but Titus didn't care about shade or water when Blake was gone. He cared about the gate. He'd be sitting at the gate with his ears up and his jaw set, a hundred thirty pounds of black muscle and devotion aimed at the place where Blake's truck usually appeared, and he would sit there until Blake came back or the sun went down, whichever came first, and if the sun went down first he would sit there in the dark.

He checked his phone. Eleven percent. He could call Cody. Cody would come. Cody would come from wherever Cody was — the clubhouse, Lisa's apartment, the bar in Oildale where he'd been spending Tuesday nights since the Vagos thing settled down — and Cody would show up in the piece-of-shit Dodge Ram with the cracked windshield and the eight-ball shift knob and he'd lean out the window and say something like "The fuck are you doing out here, primo?" and Blake would get in and they'd drive back to Bakersfield with the windows down because the Ram's AC had been broken since Cody bought it and Cody had never fixed it because Cody didn't fix things. Cody used things until they stopped working and then he got new things or he did without. That was the difference between them. Blake fixed. Cody replaced. Blake maintained. Cody burned.

He didn't call Cody. Eleven percent wasn't enough for a call and the wait and another call when Cody got lost, which Cody would, because Cody could find a bar in a blackout but couldn't find the Grapevine on a map.

He smoked. The heat pressed on him and the pressing was the only company he had.

His mother's name was Diane. She worked at a title company in Bakersfield and she had worked at the title company since Blake was six years old and she would work at the title company until she retired or died, whichever came first, and Blake suspected it would be a tie. She was a woman who had organized her life around the absence of the man who was supposed to be in it.

Blake's father left when Blake was four. Not dramatically. He left the way a man leaves a job he's been meaning to quit — bureaucratically, with a forwarding address instead of a goodbye. He went to Reno. Something about Reno collected the absent fathers of the Central Valley like a drainage ditch collects runoff — passively, without effort, by being the lowest point.

Diane raised Blake alone in a two-bedroom on Brundage Lane. She didn't drink. She didn't date. She went to work, came home, watched Jeopardy!, went to bed. On Sundays she took Blake to the Church of Christ on Union Avenue and sat in the third row and sang the hymns from memory because the hymnals had been stolen in 1994 and the congregation never replaced them. She believed in God the way she believed in the title company — as a structure that existed whether she understood it or not, that processed transactions she couldn't see, that would be there on Monday whether she showed up or not.

She had a brother. Mickey.

Mickey was the weather in the family. Everything else — Diane's quietness, the title company, the Church of Christ, the Jeopardy! — was climate. Stable. Predictable. Dry. Mickey was the storm that came through three or four times a year and rearranged everything and left and the rearranging was sometimes generous — the trips, the cash, the Hawaii vacation — and sometimes not.

Mickey was a Teamster. He was also a Vietnam vet and a man who had connections to people in Oakland and Newark who did not appear on any organizational chart. He had hands like catcher's mitts and a voice like a diesel turning over cold. He bent the world to fit himself and the world let him because the alternative was getting bent.

Blake and Cody grew up in Mickey's gravity. Cody was Mickey's son — not by blood but by proximity and by temperament. Cody's mother, Patricia, lived in Delano and worked at a packing shed and carried the hunted quietness of a woman who had learned that being noticed was dangerous. Cody spent more time at Mickey's house than at his own. By the time he was fourteen he could ride a motorcycle, strip a carburetor, and read a room for threat level faster than most adults.

Blake watched from the edges. He was always watching from the edges. At Mickey's barbecues, at the club gatherings Mickey dragged them to, at the kitchen tables where men spoke in codes Blake was learning to translate.

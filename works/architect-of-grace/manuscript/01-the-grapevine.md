# CHAPTER 01 // THE GRAPEVINE

## MANUSCRIPT

The radiator blew at mile marker sixty-three on the northbound 5 and Blake pulled the Silverado onto the shoulder and watched the steam come through the hood in a white column that bent east in the wind and dissolved against the brown hills.

September. Hundred and twelve degrees on the Grapevine. The asphalt shimmered in both directions and the shimmer made the road look wet and the wetness was a lie the Valley told every summer — the promise of water where there was no water, the mirage that kept you driving toward something that wasn't there.

He turned the key off. The engine ticked as the metal contracted. The cab was already hot and getting hotter. He had no water. He had half a pack of Marlboro Reds and a phone with eleven percent battery and an expired AAA card in the glovebox he'd been meaning to renew since March.

He got out. The heat hit him like opening an oven door. The shoulder gravel crunched under his boots — Tony Lamas, square toe, the left sole separating at the heel. He walked to the front of the truck and looked at the steam still threading up from under the hood and didn't open it because opening a blown radiator at a hundred and twelve degrees was something a man did once and then never did again and Blake had done it once, in Oildale, when he was nineteen, and the scar on the inside of his left forearm was still there, shiny and tight, a strip of skin that had been cooked and had healed into something smoother than the skin around it, as if the burning had simplified it.

He lit a cigarette. He stood on the shoulder of Interstate 5 at the foot of the Tehachapi Mountains and smoked and watched the trucks come up the grade in the slow lane, loaded, the diesels working. Nobody stopped. He didn't expect them to.

He thought about Titus. The dog would be pacing by now. Blake had left him in the yard with the water trough full and the shade tarp staked over the concrete pad, but Titus didn't care about shade or water when Blake was gone. He cared about the gate. He'd be sitting at the gate with his ears up and his jaw set, a hundred thirty pounds of black muscle and devotion aimed at the place where Blake's truck usually appeared, and he would sit there until Blake came back or the sun went down, whichever came first, and if the sun went down first he would sit there in the dark.

The truck that pulled over was a Ford F-150, white, older model, a work truck with scratches on the bed rails and a toolbox bolted behind the cab. It came up the shoulder slow and stopped thirty feet ahead of the Silverado and sat there with the hazards on and the engine running.

The driver's door opened. A man got out. Short. Dodgers hat pulled low. Work boots. Jeans. A T-shirt that had been white at some point. He was brown-skinned and compact and he moved with the unhurried efficiency of someone who had spent his life carrying things heavier than himself.

"You need a ride?"

The accent was there. Not heavy. The consonants softened at the edges.

Blake looked at him. The man's face was open. Not smiling — open. A door left that way on purpose.

"Radiator blew," Blake said.

"I can see that. I'm heading to Bakersfield. I can drop you."

Blake locked the Silverado. He walked to the Ford. The passenger side. Opened the door. The cab was cool. On the back seat, in a white cardboard box, was a cake. Pink frosting.

"I'm Jaime," the man said. He checked his mirror and merged back onto the highway.

"Blake."

The truck accelerated up the grade. Blake watched the Silverado shrink in the side mirror and felt the quiet irritation of a man rescued against his will, forced to sit in the debt of it for sixty miles.

"The cake," Blake said.

"Yeah. Tres leches. From a bakery in East LA. You can't get a good one in Bakersfield." Jaime said this with a small, private smile aimed at the windshield.

"You go to church?" Jaime asked.

The question landed in the cab like a dropped tool. Blake looked at him.

"Sometimes," Blake said.

This was the truest thing he'd said in the truck. Sometimes. He lived in the sometimes — it was where he slept, where he kept his things, where he went when he had nowhere else to go.

Jaime nodded. He didn't push it. He didn't ask which church or offer his own. He just nodded and drove and the nod was the most infuriating thing about him — the nod said I hear you, the nod said that's enough. Blake would have preferred the sell. The sell he knew how to refuse.

Jaime pulled into a Chevron on White Lane and Rosedale Highway. Blake opened the door. The heat again. He grabbed his duffel.

"You good?" Jaime said.

"I'm good."

He got out. Closed the door. Stood on the concrete apron of the gas station. The Ford sat there with the engine running and the tres leches cake in the back and Jaime behind the wheel with his hands at ten and two, waiting for Blake to walk away before he pulled out. Making sure he was good.

Blake lifted his chin. A half-nod. The Bakersfield thank you — the one that costs nothing and means just enough.

Jaime lifted his hand off the wheel. One finger. The Bakersfield you're welcome.

As Jaime shifted the Ford into gear, Blake saw Jaime's jaw tighten once, a hard lateral clench, before he released his ten-and-two grip and let the truck roll forward. It was a small movement, a momentary betrayal of the stillness he'd maintained for sixty miles.

The Ford merged into the traffic and disappeared south.

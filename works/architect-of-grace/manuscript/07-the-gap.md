# CHAPTER 07 // THE GAP

## MANUSCRIPT

The Valley heat broke on the first Tuesday of October. It gave way to a bruised alkaline dusk that spilled down from the Tehachapis and settled over White Lane.

Salty's Barbecue sat set back from the street in a gravel lot, announced by a neon pig that buzzed with a bad ballast and threw flickering pink light against the sky. The dining room was a tunnel of faux-wood paneling and scuffed linoleum. The brisket was bottom of the barrel — chopped fine and piled onto brown paper boats that went translucent with grease.

Blake sat in a plastic booth in the back corner where he could see the door. He had been there for twenty minutes. In front of him on a thin paper boat sat a slab of chopped brisket. He hadn't touched it.

He kept his hands on his thighs beneath the table. Palms down. Fingers loose. His left boot scraped the linoleum. The duct tape was gone. The sole flapped loose.

Across the table sat Rick. Rick was not eating. He held a white Styrofoam cup of black coffee in both his thick scarred hands. He was not wearing his cut. A faded gray thermal shirt buttoned at the collar. The absence of the Death's Head made the man look older.

The bell above the glass door rang.

The door pushed open and Cody came into the restaurant. Jeans. Scuffed work boots. Black T-shirt. Over the shirt hung the heavy leather of his prospect cut, the bottom rocker reading California curving along his lower back.

Rick watched him approach. His eyes locked onto the center of the boy's back. The empty space between the shoulder blades. The blank altar waiting for the winged skull.

Cody pulled out the third chair and sat. "Traffic on the 99 backed up past Ming," Cody said. "Walked from the clinic."

Cody pulled the paper boat across the table and ate. He ate rapidly. The knuckles of his right hand were swollen purple and yellow. He smeared the grease across his knuckles, filled a Styrofoam cup from a plastic pitcher of sweet tea, and drank half of it in one swallow.

Cody set the cup down. He looked at Rick's thermal shirt. He looked at Blake's face. The flat, direct focus snapped into place. He stopped chewing.

"So," Cody said, leaning back in the creaking chair. "Who are we hitting?"

Rick set his Styrofoam cup on the table. He placed his hands flat on the faux-wood grain. Palms down. Open.

"We're not hitting anybody, kid," Rick said. "I'm not riding anymore. I'm done with the table. I'm stepping down."

Cody didn't move. Cody stared at the president of the charter, his brain attempting to process a sequence of words that violated the fundamental laws of his universe.

Blake leaned forward. He brought his open hands up from his lap and placed them on the table.

"Me too," Blake said. Quiet. "It's over."

Cody's hands contracted. The fingers curled inward, fingernails digging into callouses. The knuckles turned bone-white as the fists locked against the table. His jaw clenched. A single violent tremor jumped beneath the skin.

He stared at Rick. Then at Blake.

"What is this?" Cody whispered.

"It's a graveyard, son." The gravel was gone from Rick's voice. An aging man looking at a ruined boy. "The patch, the table, the brotherhood. It's a lie. We're telling you so you don't take the Cut on Saturday."

Cody shoved the table. The plastic legs shrieked against the linoleum. The paper boat slid. The pitcher of sweet tea tipped, spilling amber liquid across the surface and soaking the brown napkins.

Cody stood. His chest heaving. The leather cut expanding on his shoulders.

"You don't get to do this," Cody said. Not loud. Pressurized.

"Cody—" Blake started.

"You built this." Cody pointed a trembling, grease-stained finger at Rick. "You put this leather on my back. You told me it was the only thing that mattered. The only thing." His voice cracked. "You made me. I gave you my skeleton. I gave you my hands. I bled for that table because you said it meant we were brothers. And you're walking out the back door?"

"We're trying to open the door for you too," Blake said.

Cody turned the dark eyes on his cousin. He looked at Blake's open hands on the table.

"You're cowards," Cody breathed. "Both of you. You're dead."

He turned his back on them — the blank space of the prospect cut flashing under the fluorescent lights — and stormed toward the exit. He hit the glass door with both hands. The bell tore loose from its bracket and clattered to the floor.

Cody hit the concrete of White Lane and walked south. His boots struck the pavement in a heavy, driving rhythm, beating against the white static of rage detonating in his skull. The traffic moved past him in a blur of headlights and exhaust, the arterial blood of Bakersfield pumping through the cooling October air.

Cowards. Traitors. Dead men. The words looped, fueling the furnace. He had given them his skeleton. He had stood against the steel door for hours, surviving the cold and the empty house on Maryland Avenue because the table was the rock. The brotherhood was the only vow that mattered. Now the rock was smoke.

He crossed the first intersection where the streetlights buzzed with current. Two blocks. Three. The streetlights spaced out. He entered the dark gap — the stretch of sidewalk where the commercial illumination failed and the shadows pooled thick against the block walls. He walked past the darkened windows of the Kern Valley Veterinary Clinic. The handwritten V made of surgical tape clung to the sign. He walked over the square of concrete where he had carried the bleeding dog in his arms. The memory of Lisa's hands displaced the static in his head. Steady. Precise. Packing gauze into the torn shoulder of an animal chained to a cinder block and built to lose.

Four blocks south of Salty's, the anger ran out of fuel. The adrenaline spike shattered, leaving a sudden hollow quiet in his chest. The armor of the Cut on his shoulders turned impossibly heavy. The alkaline dust settled over him.

He stopped. The October wind moved through the sparse branches of an oleander bush against the chain-link fence. His chest rose and fell. The fury drained out.

They were right. The wolves were gone. The table was dead. The roof was not worth holding.

Cody looked down at his hands. Slowly, the white strain in his knuckles faded. He forced his fingers to uncurl. The tight fists released, the tension bleeding out of his forearms. He opened his hands, letting them fall loose at his sides. His jaw unlocked. His shoulders dropped an inch. The heavy leather of the prospect cut loosened.

He shifted his weight to the ball of his left foot. He planted his heel on the concrete. The friction of the rubber sole against the pavement made a small, definitive scrape. His right calf engaged. His torso rotated. His shoulders squared, redirecting his body away from the dark gap.

He turned his heel. Facing north. Looking back up White Lane toward the neon pig, toward the table, toward the bread and the men who loved him enough to break his heart.

He took a breath to begin the walk back.

The flat crack died instantly in the heavy air.

His knees surrendered to the concrete. His shoulder struck the pavement. He did not reach for the wound. He did not fight the descent. The blood pooled instantly, black in the shadows, running into the cracks of the pavement. The scent of yeast from the panadería mixed with the sudden, sharp copper of his own blood.

Next to the pooling shadow, resting on the grit of the pavement, his hands lay completely open.

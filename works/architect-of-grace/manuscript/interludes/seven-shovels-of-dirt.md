# INTERLUDE // SEVEN SHOVELS OF DIRT

*Placement: between Ch 2 (The Prospect) and Ch 3 (The Island).*

## MANUSCRIPT

Rick dug alone.

The shovel was a round-point he'd taken from a county maintenance shed on the 178 six months ago. Ash handle. The grain had split where the lacquer peeled and the wood bit into the meat of his palms with every stroke. He didn't wear gloves. Hadn't worn gloves for anything since 1987 and he wasn't going to start now, digging a trench behind a condemned Teen Challenge facility on the south side of Bakersfield.

The trench was four feet deep and thirty feet long. The plan was a root cellar. Water storage. A place to put people when the people had nowhere left to go.

The shovel hit caliche at four feet. Calcium deposit that ran through the Valley subsoil like bone through meat. The blade struck it and the vibration went up the handle, through his wrists, through his forearms, and settled in the base of his skull where it woke something that had been sleeping.

The first man's name was Danny Salcido. Danny was twenty-three. Prospect.

Rick shot him in the chest with a .38 snub-nose from four feet. Danny sat down on the garage floor like a man who'd been told something confusing and needed a minute to think about it. His hands went to his chest. The rag was still in his right hand. He looked at Rick with pure incomprehension. Rick shot him again.

The shovel bit into the caliche. Rick leaned on the handle and breathed. His back was bad — two herniated discs from a wreck on the Grapevine in '94, twenty years of riding, and the kind of damage a body accumulates when it's been used as a tool for four decades without maintenance. His hands were bleeding where the ash handle had torn the calluses open. Blood mixed with dirt. A paste the color of rust.

He kept digging.

The second man was a CPA from Visalia named Gerald Fenn. Gerald wasn't in the life. Fifty-one years old. Accountant with a comb-over. Managed the books for a trucking company in Tulare that washed money for the club. Gerald decided to grow a conscience.

Rick came up behind him fast. The garrote was eighteen inches of piano wire with wooden dowel handles. He looped it over Gerald's head and pulled. Gerald dropped the briefcase. The travel mug hit the driveway. Gerald's legs kicked. His loafers scraped the concrete. One came off.

When it was done, Rick lowered the body to the driveway. Removed the garrote. Placed Gerald's loafer back on his foot. He drove the van back to Bakersfield.

Number six changed it.

His name was Martin Kessler and he was a brother. Road captain. Martin's sin was doubt. He'd started going to a church in Reedley. The table voted.

Rick rode to Reedley. Found Martin in the church parking lot, sitting in his truck with the window down, smoking a cigarette. The Bible was on the bench seat next to him, open, face down.

Martin's jaw worked once. His shoulders dropped. He didn't run.

"They voted," Martin said.

"Yeah," Rick said.

"You drew it."

"I hold the gavel, Marty. I draw everything."

Martin nodded. Took a drag. Looked at the open Bible on the seat beside him. Looked back at Rick.

"You should read that," Martin said. He pointed at the book with his cigarette. "Not tonight. Someday. When you're ready."

Rick shot him twice in the chest. Martin slumped against the driver's door. The Bible slid off the seat and landed on the floor of the cab, still open, face down. Rick reached into the truck and picked up the Bible. He put it in his jacket. Rode home. Put the Bible on his kitchen counter next to a half-empty bottle of bourbon and a loaded .45. He didn't touch any of the three for nine days.

On the tenth day he opened the Bible. It opened where Martin had left it. Romans 5. A single verse underlined in pencil: *But God demonstrates His own love for us in this: While we were still sinners, Christ died for us.*

Rick read the verse. Read it again. Read it eleven times. He sat on his kitchen floor with his back against the cabinet and the Bible in his lap and his hands — the hands that had killed six men and ordered the death of a seventh — were shaking. For the first time since he was a child, his hands were shaking.

Number seven was himself.

The seventh man he killed was the man he used to be. He drove to a storefront church in east Bakersfield with bars on the windows and a cross made of welded rebar above the door.

"I've killed six men," Rick said to the pastor. "I don't know why I'm here except that a man I killed told me to read a book and I read it and now I can't stop reading it."

The pastor reached across the table and put his hand on Rick's hand. Small hand. Rick's could have closed around it and crushed it. The pastor's hand just sat there.

"The door's unlocked," the pastor said. "You can leave whenever you want."

Rick didn't leave. He sat in that room for four hours.

Rick leaned on the shovel. The trench was six feet deep. His hands were destroyed. Blood dried in the creases of his palms, calluses gone, what was underneath pink and raw and new. The hurting was the only honest thing about his hands because his hands had done dishonest work for twenty years and the pain was the first payment on a debt he knew he could never settle.

He stood up. Picked up the shovel. The compound behind him was taking shape — corrugated steel dormitories, wire, gates. Built in Valley dirt by a man with blood on his hands and a Bible in his back pocket. The compound would hold two hundred people. Water and beds and a cafeteria and a courtyard and men on the wire with rifles and a gate that opened for anyone who needed to come in and a door that was never locked. The door was never locked because a pastor in a storefront church in east Bakersfield had said "the door's unlocked" and Rick had stayed anyway.

The staying was the thing.

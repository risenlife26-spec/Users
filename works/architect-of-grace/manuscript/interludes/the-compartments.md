# INTERLUDE // THE COMPARTMENTS

*Placement: between Ch 1 (The Grapevine) and Ch 2 (The Prospect).*

## MANUSCRIPT

Six months before the Grapevine, the white Silverado sat high in the equipment yard on Mickey Thompson tires that still smelled like rubber and dealership Armor All. Blake was behind the wheel in his orange-mirrored Oakleys and in each lens the yard and the men and the chain-link fence were warped together into a single distorted image he had no interest in examining.

Mateo shuffled across the dust in a faded ballcap pulled low. He stepped in close to the open window, resting his elbows on the door frame. His dirt-caked hands hovered right over Blake's lap.

"Back up, fool."

Mateo jerked his hands away. "Oh, perdón, jefe."

Blake's eyes went to the faint smudge of dust on the new paint. Third time late this week. "This is a new truck, pendejo. Don't be putting your dirty hands on it."

Heat flickered across Mateo's face. "Got it, jefe. It won't happen again. I'm sorry I was late. My truck was out of gas. My brother gave me ten dollars but the pump on Chester was down."

There it was. The story. What they never said was the thing that mattered: I messed up. I chose wrong. That was the rot. Nobody wanting to own the simple truth that they'd screwed up. And Blake — Blake could see the rot in every man he supervised and could not see it in the mirror he was wearing on his face. The Oakleys reflected the yard and the men and the chain-link fence but they did not reflect Blake. That was the function of the mirrored lens. The mirrored lens was the first compartment he put on every morning. It said: I see you. You don't see me.

"Say you slept in late," Blake said. His voice sharpened, finding the edge he knew how to use. "Don't give me the bullshit about gas. Tell me it was a hangover or you're fired."

Mateo stared at the ground. In the mirrored orange of Blake's Oakleys, his small figure floated.

"I was hungover," Mateo whispered.

Blake let a beat pass. There it was. The admission.

"Well, see, that doesn't work for me. Because you only told the truth once I pressed you against the wall. You tried to hide behind a story first." He looked at the foreman beside him. "I can't employ cowards or liars. You're fired."

Mateo didn't argue. He turned away across the yard.

In Blake's chest, a small tight satisfaction settled. He adjusted his Oakleys and caught his own reflection — jaw clenched, mouth in the flat line he associated with being in charge. But the reflection in the Oakleys was a reflection of a reflection — twice removed from the face, twice filtered, the image of a man who had just demanded honesty from a hungover kid while carrying a phone in his pocket with two conversations that would have ended his engagement, his reputation, and the moral authority he'd just used. The Oakleys showed Blake a man of principle. The phone showed the truth. And the phone was in the same pocket as the keys to the motel room he'd visit in four hours.

The compartments had doors. The doors had locks. Blake had been building them his whole life. He was the architect. He was the locksmith. He was the man who walked through one door and shut it behind him and opened the next, and the shutting and the opening happened so fast that no light from one room ever leaked into the other.

The phone was the most honest thing Blake owned. Because the phone didn't have compartments.

He left the motel at ten-fourteen. The woman was asleep. The room smelled like her shampoo and the carpet cleaner. He pulled the door shut behind him, lifting the handle as he always did, and walked to the Silverado in the parking lot.

Titus heard the truck before Blake killed the engine. The bark came once — deep, singular, the sound of a foundation settling — and then the gate was shaking and Blake could see the shape of him through the chain-link.

He unlatched the gate. Titus hit him chest-high, a hundred thirty pounds of muscle and need. The paws on his shoulders. The tongue on his jaw.

"Off, Titus. Off."

The dog dropped. Sat. His entire back end wagged so hard his hindquarters lifted off the concrete with each swing. Those brown eyes — wide, unblinking, locked on Blake's face with an intensity that had nothing to do with hunger and everything to do with the fact that Blake had been gone for sixteen hours and the dog had been counting every one.

Blake scratched behind his ears. The hundred-thirty-pound body went slack with something that could only be called trust.

The motel room was in one compartment. The yard crew was in another. Sarah was in a third. But the dog didn't live in any of them. The dog lived in the space between — the hallway that connected every room, the corridor that Blake walked through twenty times a day pretending the doors on either side were sealed. The dog knew the hallway. The dog slept in it. The dog was the only living thing that saw Blake move from room to room and never asked which one was real.

Blake sat on the step with the dog's head on his boot and the Valley dark around him and the stars coming out over the oil fields to the north. The phone buzzed in his pocket. He didn't look at it.

The dog breathed. The stars turned. The compartments held.

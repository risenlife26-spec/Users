## INTERLUDE: THE GATE

Grace is not a word. Grace is a physical event.

It doesn't arrive through the ear. Doesn't arrive through the book, though the book describes it the way a map describes a country — accurately, usefully, and without any of the wind. Grace arrives the way a child arrives: through blood, through tearing, through a passage so narrow and violent that the body delivering it is certain it won't survive. And then the body survives. And the thing that has arrived is alive and wailing and completely uninterested in how much it cost to bring it here.

The fields go to powder in September. The ditches run dry and smell of alkali. The almond trees stand in rows like crucifixes driven into the hardpan. Dust lifts from the shoulders of the road in curtains so fine they look like the land breathing out what it's been holding in all summer.

This is the Valley. This is where grace lives. Not in the cathedral. Not in the steeple. Grace lives in the dirt where the dirt has been stripped of everything that was supposed to make it productive. The stripping left it alkaline and bare. And the bareness is the condition. You can't receive what you're still holding.

Blake stood at the gate with his wallet out.

He'd been standing there for ten years. The gate was open. The sign above it said what it had always said: Free Admittance. All Must Enter Free of Charge. And every time it was his turn to walk through, he reached into his pocket and pulled out his wallet and tried to pay.

The attendant didn't take money. Never had. Empty hands. He watched Blake fumble with his bills and his coins and his receipts and his excuses and his clean boots and his DUIs and his grow room. Blake held all of it up.

The attendant looked at the portfolio. Looked at Blake.

The fee is free. Put your wallet away.

But Blake couldn't put his wallet away. He was a laborer. He paid his debts. You worked for what you got. You earned your place. You stood in line and paid the price and the paying was the proof you deserved to be there.

Blake wanted to be free. And the only way he knew how was to pay.

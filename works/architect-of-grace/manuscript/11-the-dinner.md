# CHAPTER 11 // THE DINNER

## MANUSCRIPT

Elena had set the table for six.

The apartment was small — two bedrooms, second floor, the balcony with the potted rose she'd brought from Fernando's garden. The kitchen opened onto a living room that opened onto a dining table that was too large for the space. Elena had bought it at a yard sale in Oildale because the table seated eight and Elena believed in feeding more people than she had chairs for. The table was set with mismatched plates and paper napkins folded into triangles and glasses that didn't match and the food was already out — the rice, the beans, the tortillas wrapped in a cloth, the salsa verde in a ceramic bowl Elena's mother had thrown at Fernando once and Elena had glued back together because Elena did not throw things away. She repaired them.

The blue-rimmed porcelain, a survivor of her grandmother's border crossing in a feed sack, anchored the arrangement. One plate bore a jagged, epoxy-sealed scar.

Lisa arrived offering a foil-wrapped plate of conchas and a six-dollar bottle of Vons wine, its label scuffed at the edges.

Blake and Sarah arrived at 6:20. Sarah stepped out of the Silverado in a tailored silk blouse, her spine rigidly straight, her steps clicking a measured tempo on the driveway concrete. She carried a chilled bottle of Sancerre and a twenty-two-dollar Anthropologie candle. At the table, Elena directed the seating. Sarah sat with her back hovering one inch from the vinyl chair. She adjusted her candle, centering it perfectly between the salsa verde bowl and the Sancerre, aligning its base with the grain of the oak table. Her gaze swept over Lisa's scuffed wine bottle, her lips pressing into a bloodless line. She drew her knife and severed her tortilla into four identical, geometric quarters.

Jaime sat at the head of the table. He folded his hands. Right hand over left. The right hand — the hand that held the steering wheel at ten and two, the hand that had purchased a Taurus 856 from Kern Firearms in September.

"Lord, thank you for this table," Jaime said. "For Elena's hands that made this food. For the people sitting here. For the ones who aren't. For Cody."

The knife in Sarah's hand halted. She stared at the quartered bread. Her breathing turned shallow, her shoulders locking into a rigid brace as she confronted a variable she could not categorize.

Blake lowered his fork. The tines dug into his palm. He did not loosen his grip.

Elena passed the rice to Jaime. Jaime passed the rice to Blake. The killer feeding the bereaved through his own hands. Blake took the bowl. The weight of it. The warmth of it. He spooned rice onto his plate and passed the bowl to Sarah and the passing was mechanical, the arms working while the mind burned.

They ate. The food was simple and good. Rice and beans and tortillas and salsa verde from the repaired bowl. The tres leches cake waited on the counter. Jaime ate with his right hand. Blake watched the hand lift the fork and lower the fork and lift the fork and the watching was unbearable and Blake did it anyway because the watching was the only thing keeping the gavel from swinging.

"Blake," Elena said. "Lisa was telling me about the dog. Penny. The one Cody brought in."

The name hit the table. Cody. Elena said it the way she said everything — directly, without the flinch that other people attached to the names of the dead. Cody was a fact. Cody had existed. Elena did not edit facts.

"She's doing well," Lisa said. Her voice was steady. Clinical. The floor holding. "She's put on twelve pounds. The shoulder healed clean. She sleeps in the recovery kennel but she follows me around the clinic during the day. She won't let me out of her sight."

"A bait dog," Elena said, shaking her head. "What kind of person chains a dog to a cinder block?"

Blake looked at Jaime. Jaime was looking at his plate. His jaw worked once — the lateral clench — and released. The bruised shadows under his eyes were deeper than they'd been at Blake's kitchen table. The compact strength continuing to erode from the inside.

"The same kind of person who chains a boy to a porch," Blake said.

The table went quiet. Elena looked at him. Lisa looked at him. Sarah's knife stopped. Jaime didn't look up.

"I'm sorry," Blake said. "I don't know why I said that."

Elena looked at him. Not with the look she used when she was deciding whether to be angry — that look had a jaw behind it. This look was calibrating. Her eyes moved from Blake to Jaime and back to Blake, and what she saw did not fit any category she had a name for. She let it go. She picked up the rice bowl and held it toward Jaime and said, "Más arroz," and Jaime took the bowl and the moment passed over the table the way a cloud passes over the Temblor Range — there and gone and the light is different afterward but nobody says why.

He knew exactly why he'd said it. The gavel knew. The .38 knew. The notebook in his back pocket knew. Every object in Blake's architecture was vibrating at the same frequency and the frequency was the sound of a man sitting across a dinner table from the man who killed his cousin and eating rice from the same bowl.

Elena served the cake.

She cut it into squares and placed them on small plates and passed them around the table. The tres leches was soft and white and soaked through with the three milks and the sweetness was the sweetness of the cake from the Grapevine, the cake from the baby shower, the cake that had survived the grade in the back seat of a Ford driven by a man with his hands at ten and two.

Blake pushed back from the table. "I need some air," he said. "Excuse me."

He walked through the living room. Out the front door. Down the stairs. Into the parking lot.

The Silverado was there. The glovebox was there. The .38 was there. Two blocks south, the dark gap on White Lane was there — the power-washed rectangle on the concrete, the oleander bush, the chain-link fence.

Blake stood in the parking lot and looked up at the apartment. Second floor. The balcony with the potted rose. Through the sliding glass door he could see the table — the mismatched plates, the paper napkins, the glued bowl, the tres leches cake. He could see Jaime clearing the plates. He could see Elena laughing at something Lisa said. He could see Sarah sitting with her hands folded, watching the door Blake had walked through.

He could go up the stairs. He could knock on the door. He could do it now — with witnesses, with the children in the next room, with the tres leches cake on the counter.

He stood in the parking lot. The November air. The oleander hedge along the fence. The stars over the Valley, dim through the haze.

He didn't go up the stairs. He didn't open the glovebox.

He got in the truck and drove home. Alone. With the gun and the poems and the fists and the sweetness of the cake still on his tongue.

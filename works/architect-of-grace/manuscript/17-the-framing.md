# CHAPTER 17 // THE FRAMING

## MANUSCRIPT

Jaime turned himself in on a Monday morning in January.

He drove to the Kern County Sheriff's station on Norris Road in the white F-150 with the engine he maintained every three thousand miles and the tires he rotated every six. He parked. He turned off the engine. He sat in the truck for eleven minutes.

The prayer was the same prayer Jaime had been praying since the bathroom floor, since Blake had held his hand:

*Let it hold. Whatever comes. Let the floor hold.*

He walked inside. He told the desk sergeant his name. He told him he was there to make a statement about a homicide on White Lane. They put him in a room. He told them. All of it. The stone. The gun from Rosedale. The gap. The turning. The open hands. The one round. The driving home.

\*   \*   \*

Blake drove to Rick's compound that evening.

The gate was open. The courtyard was empty. The flagpole stood without a flag. The bunkhouse windows were lit.

Rick was in the chapel. The converted barn. The pews were folding chairs. The cross on the wall was the mesquite cross Rick had made — rough, unsanded, the bark still on the edges, the wood the color of the Valley dirt it had grown in.

Rick was sitting in the front row. Not praying. Just sitting. His hands on his knees. The scarred knuckles. The thick fingers. The hands that had held the gavel and the .38 and Danny Salcido's shoe and Gerald Fenn's loafer and Martin Kessler's Bible and Blake's arm and a hundred crying boys' shoulders and a thousand cups of styrofoam coffee and the weight of seven dead men.

Blake sat next to him. The folding chair creaked. The barn was quiet.

Titus was in the aisle between the chairs, lying on the concrete floor, his chin on his paws, his eyes moving between the two men.

"The .38," Blake said. "The one I left on the hood."

"In the office. Drawer."

"Keep it."

Rick looked at him. The X-ray eyes. The scar along the jaw like a seam.

"You sure?"

"I'm sure. I don't need the weight."

Rick nodded. The nod of a structure holding. The nod of a man who had watched Blake walk through the gate three months ago carrying the full weight of the gavel and had watched Blake set pieces of it down — the gun on the hood, the poem in the notebook, the fist unclenched on the bathroom tile — and was now watching Blake set down the last piece, which was the need for the weight itself.

They sat in the chapel. The evening light came through the barn's high windows and fell on the mesquite cross and the light did something with the grain of the wood that made the grain look like it was moving — not growing, not changing, just breathing. The way wood breathes when the humidity shifts. The way a body breathes when it has decided to stay.

Blake thought about the postcard. *I'm sorry for more than I know how to put on a card.* He thought about Lisa's hand on the tile. He thought about Cody's open hands on the sidewalk. He thought about Jaime's grip on the bathroom floor. He thought about Elena's hand on the knot between Jaime's shoulder blades. He thought about Sarah's hands interlaced in the clinic chair, the filing system's last gesture.

He thought about his own hands.

He opened them.

Palms up on his knees. Fingers spread. The way Cody's hands had opened on White Lane. The way Lisa's hand had been flat on the clinic floor. The way Martin Kessler's Bible had been open on his chest.

Open. The posture of a man putting something down.

Titus lifted his head from the floor. The dog looked at Blake's open hands. The dog got up. Walked to Blake. Put his head in Blake's lap. The weight of it — five pounds of skull and jaw and trust. The heaviest thing Blake had ever held. The lightest thing Blake had ever held. The two weights were the same weight and the sameness was the point.

Rick didn't say anything. The chapel was quiet. The cross was on the wall. The light was in the wood. The dog was in Blake's lap. The hands were open.

Outside, the Valley was settling into its evening shape. The almond orchards darkening. The derricks nodding. The packing sheds lit for the night shift. Somewhere on White Lane, the panadería was dark and the tire shop was dark and the V was taped to the sign and the streetlights cast their orange cones on the asphalt and the gaps between the cones were dark and in the gaps the memory of two people who had died with their hands open lived in the concrete the way water lives underground — unseen, unfailing, feeding the roots of whatever grows next.

Blake sat in the chapel with his hands open and the dog in his lap and the man who had killed seven people beside him and the cross on the wall and the light in the wood and the floor underneath all of it.

The floor held.

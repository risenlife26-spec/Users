# CHAPTER 06

## The Call

Patricia enjoyed the fact that her son lived on the rugged edge.

It was a structural convenience. His chaos took the focus off her own shortcomings and placed it squarely on him. She was emotionally stirred by the sight of his suffering — she would weep over the bruised jaw, the arrests, the county stints — but Cody had known since he was fourteen that the weeping was theatrical. A person looking at a stray, abandoned puppy on the side of the road. You toss it some food. You grieve for its condition. But you don’t take it home with you.

Lord knows what it will do to the carpets. Not to mention, you don’t know what that thing’s got.

So you sigh. You say, “Well, in another life.” And you walk away. Leaving the puppy to be raised by wolves.

She didn’t care. As long as she had his delinquency and his criminal adult history to cash in as emotional capital — able to spend it on being late for Sunday school, a reason for missing an electric bill. The opportunities to exploit her son’s condition were the canvas. The specific scenarios where she employed the tactic were the paint. It is astonishing what a mother will sanctify just to keep avoiding what she owes. Patricia had sanctified her son’s suffering the way a landlord sanctifies a condemned building — not by fixing it but by collecting rent from the decay.

She had never thought the kid would live past twenty anyway.

There had been a mother on Maryland Avenue, once, who used to love him. A mother who used to look at him and see her little boy. But the contrast now was too sharp, the gap too far a chasm. Now she looked at him and saw only a man swirling the drain. She would pay his cell phone bill. She would take his calls and collect his mail. She would enjoy his sporadic visits and placate his girlfriends, standing passively by, just waiting for the bowl to refill with water.

*

Rick sat in the dark of his kitchen in Oildale with the phone still warm in his hand and thought about the wolves.

He was the alpha of the pack she had left the boy to.

The screen had gone black ten minutes ago but Blake’s voice was still ringing in the quiet house. If we let him take it while we’re walking out the back door, we’re feeding him to it. We’re letting it eat him.

Rick set the phone face down on the Formica table. He rubbed his face with both hands. The hands were wide, thick with calcium deposits from decades of breaking and healing. These were the hands that held the gavel. These were the hands that had taken Cody in, handed him a prospect rocker, and taught him how to pack a wound, how to clear a jam, how to enforce a territorial line.

He hadn’t adopted the boy. He had weaponized him.

The silence in the house was not the cinder-block silence of the clubhouse — the silence that absorbed sound to keep secrets in. This was the brittle silence of a room where a man is alone with his own ledger. Rick knew this silence. He had lived with it for three months, on his knees on linoleum, reading the letters of a man who used to hold the coats of the men who threw the stones. Paul understood the gavel. Paul had been the gavel. And the road to Damascus had knocked Paul flat the way the kitchen floor had knocked Rick flat, and the flatness was not punishment. The flatness was the first honest position either man had held.

On the table next to a half-empty mug of black coffee sat a Bible. Imitation leather. Spine cracked. He had stolen it from a motel nightstand in Barstow three months ago because stealing a Bible felt like the exact kind of joke his life had become. He started reading it on the toilet. Then at the table. Then on his knees on the linoleum floor.

While we were still weak, at the right time Christ died for the ungodly.

The ungodly were his congregation. Rick could see that now. The men at the table, the prospects at the door, the women who waited and the children who grew up in the noise of a house where the father’s authority was the gavel and the gavel was the law and the law was loyalty and loyalty was the only virtue and breaking it was the only sin. The ungodly were Rick’s flock. He had pastored them in leather. He had served communion in Budweiser cans. He had preached the gospel of sealed-out mercy for twenty years and the congregation had believed him because the congregation had no other church.

Rick looked at the dark window over the sink. He didn’t see his own reflection. He saw Martin Kessler in the sodium light.

July 2014. Tuesday night. Valley heat refusing to break even at midnight. A church parking lot in Reedley. Martin had been the sergeant-at-arms for the charter — built like a bank safe, vicious, unshakeable. Until his daughter got sick and Martin ended up in a storefront Pentecostal church weeping into the carpet. Martin found the floor. A week later he asked for a sit-down with Rick.

I’m out, brother, Martin said. Standing by the bumper of his truck under the yellow lights. I can’t wear the patch anymore.

Rick remembered the sound his boots made on the asphalt. Martin saw him step out of the shadows and didn’t run. Didn’t drop his Bible. Didn’t reach for the knife he still carried in his boot. Martin looked at Rick and his shoulders dropped. He opened his hands.

The door’s unlocked, brother, Martin whispered.

Two hollow-points. The sound flat and heavy, echoing off the stucco strip mall. Martin fell backward. His blood pooled black under the yellow lights.

Rick had stood over the body and felt nothing. The Cut absorbed the shock. The Cut did what it was built to do — sealed out the mercy. Kept Rick safe from the vulnerability of the open hand.

Now, sitting in his kitchen, Rick knew the circle had closed. He was Martin Kessler. He was the apostate. He had found the floor. And he knew exactly what the club did to men who found the floor.

*

Tomorrow night at six o’clock he was going to walk into Salty’s Barbecue and sit down across from a boy whose mother had left him on a porch, a boy who had offered his uncompromised soul to the club because the club was the only thing that had ever let him inside. And Rick was going to tell him the brotherhood was a lie.

He knew the physics. You try to pull a true believer off the altar, they bite. Cody was not Blake. Blake had compartments. Blake could fold. Cody ran at a hundred percent in one direction and the direction was whatever direction Cody was pointed. If you told him the vestment was a shroud — that the father figures who raised him from the porch were wolves — Cody wouldn’t walk away. He would explode. He would report it to the table. The table would vote. And Rick would be hunted by the wolves he had trained.

The fear spiked in his chest. His fists clenched on the Formica. The reflex — the urge to lock the doors, load the magazines, call a meeting, reassert control — flared in his blood.

Rick looked at the open Bible.

He forced his fingers to uncurl. He left his hands open on the table. Palms up. He let the ghost of Martin Kessler sit in the empty chair across from him.

He picked up his phone. Scrolled to Cody’s name. Hit dial.

Two rings. The background noise of a bar — billiard balls, a jukebox playing something heavy, the hum of fifty people drinking on a Wednesday night.

“Rick.” Crisp. Immediate. The voice of a prospect who answers on the second ring because the second ring is the law.

“You busy?”

“Just at the spot in Oildale. You need me somewhere?”

No hesitation. No asking why. Just tell me where to be. The blind purity of the boy’s devotion made Rick close his eyes.

“Tomorrow night,” Rick said. He kept the gravel in his voice — the tone of the president — because if he softened it now Cody would know something was wrong. “Six o’clock. Salty’s BBQ on White Lane. Meet me and Blake.”

“Salty’s.” Filing the logistics. “Just the three of us?”

“Just the three of us.” Rick paused. “No cuts. Street clothes.”

A fractional silence. Telling a prospect to leave the vest at home was a disruption of the liturgy. But Cody didn’t question the priest.

“Understood,” Cody said. “I’ll be there.”

“Good.”

“Rick?”

“Yeah.”

“Everything alright?” The prospect formality dropped for half a second. The puppy checking on the wolf.

Rick swallowed the stone in his throat.

“Yeah, kid,” Rick said. “Everything’s exactly how it’s supposed to be.”

He hung up.

He walked down the short hallway to his bedroom. His cut — the heavy leather vest with the President rocker and the winged Death’s Head — was draped over the back of a wooden chair. It smelled of highway grit and old oil and authority. It was the heaviest thing he owned.

He looked at it. He didn’t put it on. He would never put it on again.

He opened the top drawer of his nightstand. The Springfield .45. The same gun from the parking lot in Reedley. He checked the magazine. Seven rounds. One in the chamber.

He stared at the steel in his hand. Love demands the laying down of self for the furtherance of the beloved. Rick did not know the sentence. He did not have the vocabulary for it. What he had was a kitchen floor and a stolen Bible and a boy at a bar in Oildale who answered on the second ring. What he had was the knowledge that the only thing standing between Cody and the Cut was a man willing to walk into a barbecue restaurant unarmed and say the unsayable, and the man was Rick, and the saying would probably kill him.

Rick popped the magazine. Racked the slide. The chambered round ejected onto the carpet.

He placed the empty gun back in the drawer. Closed it.

Tomorrow he was going to Salty’s unarmed. He was going to sit down across from the boy he had ruined and try to be the father he should have been ten years ago, before the mother on Maryland Avenue let the water bowl run dry.

Rick turned off the kitchen light. He got on his knees on the linoleum. He opened the stolen Bible to Romans. He read until his knees ached and the words blurred and the silence in the house was no longer brittle but held him, the way the floor holds a man who has finally stopped trying to hold himself.

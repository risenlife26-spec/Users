# CHAPTER 05

## Lukewarm

Joe’s grow was a corrugated steel outbuilding off 7th Standard Road where the county line blurred into pump jacks and diesel exhaust and dirt that hadn’t grown anything on its own in forty years. The exterior was rusted at the bolts. Chain-link fence around the perimeter. A padlock on the gate that took two keys.

Inside was a jungle built on math. Sixty thousand watts of high-pressure sodium light. Four hundred plants in ten-gallon fabric pots arranged in rows on rolling tables. The light was violently orange, thick and synthetic, throwing no shadows. The ballasts hummed at a frequency that bypassed the ears and sat in the molars. Seventy-eight degrees. Forty-five percent humidity. Carbon dioxide pumped from silver tanks to push the photosynthesis past what the sun could do for free.

Blake maintained the systems. He calibrated the nutrient dosers and checked the pH levels in the reservoir tanks and fixed the PVC irrigation lines when they cracked and replaced the ballasts when they blew. He did not move the product. He did not drive it down the 99 to Los Angeles. He did not touch the money. He maintained. The room was binary — a pump pulled water or it didn’t, a circuit was closed or open — and the binary was the only honest thing in his life because it didn’t require him to choose a side.

He had been living in the straddle for three years. It is astonishing what a man will sanctify just to keep wanting what he wants. Blake had sanctified the straddle. He had made it a position — not hot, not cold, not in, not out — and the position felt like wisdom because it felt like control and control was the only prayer Blake knew how to pray.

Seven in the morning until three in the afternoon he wore a high-vis vest and laid sod for a legitimate landscaping company out of Shafter. He paid taxes. He ate lunch from a cooler in the bed of the Silverado. Nine at night until two in the morning he stood in a foil-lined garage under illegal lights growing twenty pounds of product that belonged to a club he would not patch into. He went to his mother Diane’s Church of Christ on Sundays but sat in the back row and left during the closing prayer. He had moved Sarah into a duplex in Rosedale, her quiet organizational competence functioning as an anesthetic against his own disorder, but he kept a locked compartment in the back of his skull for the woman at the vet clinic on White Lane whose hands had started an IV on a collapsed vein without looking.

He stayed fourteen feet from the center of every room. Including his own.

His left boot scraped the wet concrete as he shifted his weight. The sole of the Tony Lama had separated another half-inch since the Grapevine. Every step produced a sound — scuff-slap, scuff-slap — a metronome counting down a foundation he refused to repair. Instead of replacing the boots or taking them to be stitched, Blake had wrapped a band of silver duct tape around the instep. A patch. A half-measure to keep the thing walking without fixing it. The duct tape was the straddle made visible. The duct tape was Blake’s entire theology: hold it together. Don’t look at what’s separating. Wrap the surface. Walk.

*

Bakersfield spread across a hundred and fifty square miles of Valley floor. Four hundred thousand people. You could go years without seeing the same stranger twice.

Since the Grapevine, Blake had seen Jaime four times.

The first was at the Ewing Irrigation supply yard on Rosedale Highway. Blake buying PVC primer. Jaime walking in with a coil of drip line over his shoulder. The chin-lift. Nothing more.

The second was at a Chevron on Coffee Road. Blake at pump four. Jaime at pump seven. The Ford idling clean, the oil changed on schedule, the tires rotated. Blake’s Silverado leaking transmission fluid onto the concrete in a pattern he’d memorized and hadn’t fixed.

The third was at a taco truck in Oildale. Jaime ordering in Spanish, waiting for the food with his hands in his pockets and his weight settled and his face carrying the expression of a man who was exactly where he intended to be.

The fourth was yesterday at a Lowe’s on Panama Lane. Jaime loading bags of mulch into the bed of the white F-150. He’d looked across the parking lot, seen Blake, and given the single-finger lift. Blake had given the chin-lift back.

They never spoke. But the repetition had stopped feeling like coincidence. It felt like the Valley was narrowing — funneling the two men into the same corridor until they would have to stand in the same room and one of them would have to say something first.

Blake’s jaw tightened every time he saw the Ford. Jaime had a center. He was present in his truck, present in the dirt, present in his body — the quiet, settled gravity of a man whose foundation was holding. Blake could see it from across a parking lot. The seeing produced in his chest a pressure that started behind the sternum and radiated outward into the arms, because the man in the Dodgers hat was a mirror and the mirror showed Blake the exact dimensions of what he had spent his entire adult life avoiding: the open air. Jaime stood in the open air. He stood in it the way a man stands in his own yard — without apology, without armor, without the fourteen feet. And Blake, who had built an architecture of distance so elaborate that even his own wife lived on the other side of a closed door, could not look at Jaime without seeing the blueprint of his own cowardice rendered in negative space.

*

It was a Tuesday in November. The Valley had cooled. The orange light pressed down on Blake’s shoulders as he bled an airlock out of the main irrigation manifold. His hands were wet. His knuckles smeared with purple PVC primer. The ballasts hummed in his teeth.

His mother’s voice arrived without invitation. Diane at the kitchen table on Brundage Lane, reading aloud while Blake ate cereal before school, her finger on the page, her voice carrying the flat Bakersfield cadence that made scripture sound like directions to the hardware store.

I know your deeds, that you are neither cold nor hot. I wish you were either one or the other. So, because you are lukewarm — neither hot nor cold — I will spit you out of my mouth.

He had heard the verse a hundred times. He had filed it under the same label he filed everything Diane read at the kitchen table — background noise, the ambient hum of a religion he observed from the back row and left during the closing prayer.

But standing in the orange light with PVC primer on his hands and a separating boot sole and a compartment for the grow room and a compartment for the landscaping yard and a compartment for the woman on White Lane and a compartment for his cousin’s swollen knuckles and a compartment for the man in the Dodgers hat and a compartment for the wife who managed his chaos without being allowed inside it — standing in the straddle at eleven-forty on a Tuesday night, the verse did not sound like background noise.

It sounded like a diagnosis.

There is no reform without acknowledged failure. Blake had known this the way a man knows the foundation is cracked — he could feel it in the floors, in the doors that didn’t close right, in the windows that stuck. He had spent years rearranging the furniture of his reality, pushing the heavy pieces of memory and justification against every door, searching for a configuration that didn’t indict him. The configuration did not exist. The furniture was not the problem. The house was the problem. The house was built on the straddle and the straddle was not a foundation. It was a crack that ran from the footing to the roof and the crack was getting wider and the duct tape could not hold it.

The pressure behind his sternum spiked. Not an emotion. A physical compression — the iron walls between the compartments buckling under load. The guilt of his want for Lisa bled into the violence of Cody’s knuckles bled into the quiet of the man at the gas pump bled into the orange light and the forced growth and the duct tape holding his boot together, a series of patches over separating soles, half-measures stacked on half-measures, and the stack was collapsing.

His shoulders curled inward. His jaw locked. His hands, holding the red-handled PVC cutters, closed into tight fists. The body folding in on itself — making itself small enough to survive the vacuum.

He couldn’t breathe. The orange light smeared. The sixty-hertz hum became a roar.

Blake dropped the PVC cutters. They hit the wet concrete.

He went to his knees. The duct tape on his left boot scraped the floor. He knelt in the spilled water and the purple primer and the smell of the forced harvest. His hands were still clenched. His jaw still locked. Every reflex trying to hold the architecture together — seal the compartments, maintain the straddle, keep the fourteen feet.

He fought it. He fought the fists. He forced his fingers to uncurl — one at a time, each one a door opening onto a room he had kept locked for years. He laid his hands flat on his thighs. Palms up. He dropped his shoulders. He let the perimeter collapse.

The air hit him.

Not a choir. Not a light. The detonation of a held breath — the crack in the chest when the thing you’ve been gripping lets go and the letting go is so sudden and so total that the body doesn’t know what to do with the space. The heavy weight settling. The floor beneath his knees catching him the way bedrock catches a footer.

He was not holding himself up. The floor was holding him.

Blake knelt in the grow room and wept. It was not cinematic. It was ugly and absolute — the weeping of a man who has been pressing against a door for twenty-six years and is finally dragged through it. Not the clean kind. Not the baptism-video kind. The kind where a man’s failures are not the minor missteps of a distracted life but structural collapses, and the man kneeling in the rubble of his own architecture has finally run out of ways to pretend the building is standing. And in the middle of it, arriving without invitation — his mother’s voice had arrived earlier — came the specific sound he had been leaving before it finished for as long as he could remember: twenty-three people singing “Farther Along” in the Church of Christ on Union Avenue in the flat Bakersfield cadence that made the hymn sound like directions to a place nobody was sure existed. Diane’s voice somewhere in the fourth row. The off-key certainty of people who believed the words enough to sing them badly in public. He had never made it to the end of that hymn. He was at the end of it now. He knelt with his hands open and the water soaking through his jeans and the purple primer drying on his knuckles and he let his lukewarm life burn to the ground.

*

The November air hit him when he walked out the side door. Cold and clean after the synthetic heat. The Valley sky a bruised purple, the sun long gone behind the Temblor Range.

He opened the door of the Silverado. Titus was in the passenger seat — the dog rode everywhere with him, a hundred thirty pounds of black muscle that waited in the truck while Blake did things the dog could not follow him into. The dog’s head came up. The brown eyes found Blake’s face in the dark. Titus didn’t bark. He pressed his skull forward into Blake’s hand — the massive head pushing against the palm, the weight of it, the warmth — and Blake stood there with his hand on the dog’s head and his knees still wet from the grow room floor and the November air on his face and he held the dog’s skull the way a drowning man holds a rope.

He pulled his phone from his pocket.

The clarity in his chest was terrifying. The compartments were gone. The walls were down. He was standing in the center of the room for the first time in his life, and standing in the center meant the straddle had to end tonight — before morning light made the fence look manageable again.

He scrolled to the contact labeled Rick. He pressed call.

Three rings.

“Yeah.” Flat. Heavy. The cinder-block room on Edison Highway.

“It’s Blake.”

“I know who it is. Reservoir holding?”

“Reservoir’s fine.” Blake looked over the cab at the purple sky. “I’m done, Rick.”

Five seconds of silence. The faint clink of a bottle.

“Done with what.” Half an octave lower.

“The grow. The runs. The envelopes.” The truth was a rock in his throat. He forced it up. “I can’t do the middle anymore. Gave it up just now. In the room. I’m out.”

He waited for the explosion. The cold instruction. The gavel coming down.

The silence stretched. Ten seconds.

When Rick spoke, the voice didn’t sound like the gavel. It sounded stripped. An old man releasing a rope he’d been gripping for twenty years.

“You remember Martin Kessler?”

“The brother from Reedley? Got hit in the parking lot of that —”

“I’m the one who hit him.” Quiet. “Two in the chest. Parking lot of a storefront church on a Tuesday night. He found what you just found. He was gonna walk. I killed him for it.”

Blake’s open hand gripped the edge of the truck door. He stopped breathing.

“Been reading Romans,” Rick said. The granite cracked. A micro-fissure. “On my kitchen floor. Every night. Three months. Sitting at the head of that table with the gavel in my hand knowing I’m a dead man walking. Knowing the whole thing is a graveyard.” A long exhale. “You hear me, son? You’re not the only one.”

The Valley tilted. The cab of the Silverado felt impossibly small — the entire county contracting around the frequency of the call. The president of the charter and the lukewarm nephew, both standing on the same floor.

“Rick,” Blake whispered.

“Yeah.”

“We have to tell Cody.”

Silence.

“He’s prospecting,” Blake said. “You’re going to put the Cut on him Saturday. If we let him take it while we’re walking out the back door, we’re feeding him to it. We’re letting it eat him.”

“Kid’s a true believer, Blake. You try to pull a true believer off the altar, they bite. He only runs one direction.”

“Then we stand in front of him. Both of us. The blood and the gavel. Maybe it breaks the spell.”

Rick breathed into the phone. The sound of a man looking down a dark tunnel and knowing he has to walk into it.

“Salty’s Barbecue,” Rick said. His voice hardened — not the gavel anymore, a man making a stand. “Tomorrow night. Six o’clock. You get the kid there. Just the three of us. No cuts.”

“I’ll get him there.”

“Blake.”

“Yeah.”

“Don’t tell him what it’s about. He won’t come if he knows.”

“I won’t.”

The line clicked dead.

Blake lowered the phone. He stood in the November air. He looked down at his left boot. The duct tape gleamed dull silver in the ambient light. He reached down and peeled the tape off the instep in one long strip. The adhesive left a residue on the leather. The sole hung loose, unpatched, the separation complete.

He dropped the tape in the dirt.

He got in the truck. He pulled the door shut. He drove west toward White Lane, toward the intersection where the streetlights started and the dark began.
